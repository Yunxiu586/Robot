# State-Space Models

[toc]

### State-Space Representation

##### State Variables and State

The state of a dynamic system is a minimal collection of variables whose values at an initial time, together with the input applied thereafter, determine the future behavior of the system. The individual components are called **state variables**.

A minimal state description contains no redundant state coordinates. The particular choice of state variables is generally **not unique**, but all minimal realizations of the same finite-dimensional input-output behavior have the same state dimension.

For an $n$-dimensional model, the state variables are collected in the **state vector**

$$
\boldsymbol{x}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}x_1(t)&x_2(t)&\cdots&x_n(t)\end{bmatrix}^{T}
$$

The **state space** is the set of admissible values of $\boldsymbol{x}$, typically $\mathbb{R}^n$ for a real finite-dimensional model. A **state trajectory** is the curve $t\mapsto\boldsymbol{x}(t)$ generated by the state equation for a specified input and initial state.

##### State and Output Equations

A general continuous-time state-space model has the form

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u},t)\qquad \boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u},t)
$$

For a linear time-invariant system,

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=A\boldsymbol{x}+B\boldsymbol{u}\qquad \boldsymbol{y}=C\boldsymbol{x}+D\boldsymbol{u}
$$

where $A$ is the **state matrix**, $B$ is the input matrix, $C$ is the output matrix, and $D$ is the feedthrough matrix. For $\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n$, $\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^m$, and $\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^p$,

$$
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\qquad B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\qquad C\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}\qquad D\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times m}
$$

The state equation describes how the input and present state determine the state derivative. The output equation describes how the present state and input determine the output.

The term $D\boldsymbol{u}$ is absent only when there is no direct feedthrough from input to output; in particular, a strictly proper transfer function has $D=0$.

##### All-Integrator Representation

A linear time-invariant state-space model can be represented by an all-integrator simulation diagram containing **integrators, constant gains, and summing junctions**. The outputs of the integrators provide a natural choice of state variables.

For an $n$th-order differential equation, the model is first rewritten as a set of $n$ first-order equations.

Choosing the outputs of the resulting integrator chain as states gives a state-space representation directly from the signal relations in the diagram.

**eg.** Consider the first-order lag
$$
G(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{U(s)}=\frac{K}{Ts+1}
$$

Then

$$
(Ts+1)Y(s)=KU(s)
$$

and in the time domain

$$
T\dot y+y=Ku
$$

Hence

$$
\dot y=-\frac{1}{T}y+\frac{K}{T}u
$$

or equivalently, for an initial time $t_0$

$$
y(t)=y(t_0)+\int_{t_0}^{t}\left[\frac{K}{T}u(\tau)-\frac{1}{T}y(\tau)\right]d\tau
$$

### Construction of State-Space Models

##### From Physical Laws

A state-space model may be obtained directly from the governing physical laws. Choose a set of independent variables sufficient to specify the dynamic condition of the system, use the physical equations to express their first derivatives, and write the measured variables as functions of the states and inputs.

For an electrical network, Kirchhoff's voltage and current laws provide the algebraic relations needed to form the first-order state equations after the state variables have been chosen.

**eg.** Consider a circuit in which the voltage source $u$ drives an inductor $L$ in series with a parallel $R$-$C$ branch. Let $v_C$ be the voltage across the parallel branch and $i_L$ the current through the inductor. Choose

$$
x_1=v_C\qquad x_2=i_L
$$

Kirchhoff's current law gives

$$
x_2=C\dot x_1+\frac{1}{R}x_1
$$

$$
\dot x_1=-\frac{1}{RC}x_1+\frac{1}{C}x_2
$$

Kirchhoff's voltage law gives

$$
x_1+L\dot x_2=u
$$

$$
\dot x_2=-\frac{1}{L}x_1+\frac{1}{L}u
$$

Therefore

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
-\frac{1}{RC}&\frac{1}{C}\\
-\frac{1}{L}&0
\end{bmatrix}
\boldsymbol{x}
+
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
\frac{1}{L}
\end{bmatrix}u
$$

If the capacitor voltage is taken as the output,

$$
y=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}
$$

##### Internal and External Descriptions

A transfer function or input-output differential equation is an **external description** because it specifies the relation between input and output without explicitly describing the internal variables.

A state-space model is an **internal description** because it introduces the state $\boldsymbol{x}$ and specifies both the internal dynamics and the relation from state and input to output.

##### Realization

A state-space model is a **realization** of a transfer function if it has the same zero-state input-output relation. For a SISO rational transfer function, write

$$
G(s)=\frac{B(s)}{A(s)}=\frac{b_ms^m+\cdots+b_1s+b_0}{s^n+a_{n-1}s^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1s+a_0}
$$

An ordinary finite-dimensional state-space model

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=A\boldsymbol{x}+B\boldsymbol{u}\qquad \boldsymbol{y}=C\boldsymbol{x}+D\boldsymbol{u}
$$

has the transfer function $G(s)=C(sI-A)^{-1}B+D$, which is proper. Therefore a realization of this form requires $m\leq n$.

If $m<n$, $G(s)$ is **strictly proper** and $D=0$.

If $m=n$, polynomial division gives a nonzero constant feedthrough term.

If $m>n$, $G(s)$ is improper and cannot be represented by this ordinary state-space form without input derivatives or a more general system description.

A realization is **minimal** if no realization of the same transfer function has smaller state dimension. For a SISO rational transfer function, common factors of $A(s)$ and $B(s)$ must first be canceled; a realization whose order equals the degree of the reduced denominator is minimal.

### Canonical Realizations

##### SISO Transfer Function

For $m<n$ and monic $A(s)$, use the standard notation

$$
G(s)=\frac{B(s)}{A(s)}=\frac{b_{n-1}s^{n-1}+\cdots+b_1s+b_0}{s^n+a_{n-1}s^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1s+a_0}
$$

where $b_i=0$ for $i>m$.

If $m=n$,

$$
G(s)=\frac{b_ns^n+b_{n-1}s^{n-1}+\cdots+b_1s+b_0}{s^n+a_{n-1}s^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1s+a_0}
$$

polynomial division gives

$$
G(s)=b_n+\frac{(b_{n-1}-a_{n-1}b_n)s^{n-1}+\cdots+(b_1-a_1b_n)s+(b_0-a_0b_n)}{s^n+a_{n-1}s^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1s+a_0}
$$

Hence $D=b_n$, and the remaining part is realized by the same canonical constructions.

##### Controllable Canonical Form (CCF)

For both cases, use the companion matrix

$$
A_c=\begin{bmatrix}
0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\\
-a_0&-a_1&-a_2&\cdots&-a_{n-1}
\end{bmatrix}
\qquad
B_c=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\\vdots\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}
$$

**For $m<n$.**

$$
C_c=\begin{bmatrix}b_0&b_1&\cdots&b_{n-1}\end{bmatrix}
\qquad D_c=0
$$

**For $m=n$.** Define
$$
\bar b_i=b_i-a_ib_n,\qquad i=0,1,\ldots,n-1
$$

$$
C_c=\begin{bmatrix}\bar b_0&\bar b_1&\cdots&\bar b_{n-1}\end{bmatrix}
\qquad D_c=b_n
$$

**Derivation.** Introduce the intermediate variable $w$ by
$$
A(s)W(s)=U(s)
$$

$$
w^{(n)}+a_{n-1}w^{(n-1)}+\cdots+a_1\dot w+a_0w=u
$$

Choose

$$
x_1=w\qquad x_2=\dot w\qquad \cdots\qquad x_n=w^{(n-1)}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot x_1&=x_2\\
\dot x_2&=x_3\\
&\ \vdots\\
\dot x_n&=-a_0x_1-a_1x_2-\cdots-a_{n-1}x_n+u
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus
$$
A_c=\begin{bmatrix}
0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\\
-a_0&-a_1&-a_2&\cdots&-a_{n-1}
\end{bmatrix}
\qquad
B_c=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\\vdots\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}
$$
Since
$$
W(s)=\frac{U(s)}{A(s)} \qquad G(s)=\frac{B(s)}{A(s)} \qquad Y(s)=G(s)U(s)
$$

$$
Y(s)=B(s)W(s)
$$

**For $m<n$,**
$$
Y(s)=B(s)W(s)
=\left(b_{n-1}s^{n-1}+\cdots+b_1s+b_0\right)W(s)
$$
Under zero initial conditions, the differentiation property of the Laplace transform gives
$$
\mathcal{L}\left\{w^{(k)}(t)\right\}=s^kW(s)
$$
Therefore, taking the inverse Laplace transform yields
$$
y=b_0w+b_1\dot w+\cdots+b_{n-1}w^{(n-1)}
$$

$$
x_1=w\qquad x_2=\dot w\qquad \cdots\qquad x_n=w^{(n-1)}
$$

With these states, the output equation becomes
$$
y=b_0x_1+b_1x_2+\cdots+b_{n-1}x_n
$$
Hence,
$$
C_c=\begin{bmatrix}b_0&b_1&\cdots&b_{n-1}\end{bmatrix}
\qquad D_c=0
$$
**For $m=n$,** since
$$
A(s)W(s)=U(s)
$$

$$
(s^n+a_{n-1}s^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1s+a_0)W(s)=U(s)
$$

Thus
$$
s^nW(s)=U(s)-(a_{n-1}s^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1s+a_0)W(s)
$$
Since
$$
Y(s)=B(s)W(s)
=(b_{n}s^{n}+\cdots+b_1s+b_0)W(s)
$$

$$
Y(s)=b_{n}(s^{n}W(s))+(b_{n-1}s^{n-1}+\cdots+b_1s+b_0)W(s)
$$

Thus
$$
Y(s)=(b_{n-1}s^{n-1}+\cdots+b_1s+b_0)W(s)-b_n(a_{n-1}s^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1s+a_0)W(s)+b_nU(s)
$$
Define

$$
\bar b_i=b_i-a_ib_n
\qquad i=0,1,\ldots,n-1
$$

$$
Y(s)
=
\left(
\bar b_{n-1}s^{n-1}
+\cdots+
\bar b_1s
+\bar b_0
\right)W(s)
+b_nU(s)
$$

Under zero initial conditions, taking the inverse Laplace transform gives

$$
y
=
\bar b_0w
+\bar b_1\dot w
+\cdots+
\bar b_{n-1}w^{(n-1)}
+b_nu
$$

$$
x_1=w\qquad x_2=\dot w\qquad \cdots\qquad x_n=w^{(n-1)}
$$

With these states, the output equation becomes

$$
y
=
\bar b_0x_1
+\bar b_1x_2
+\cdots+
\bar b_{n-1}x_n
+b_nu
$$

Hence,

$$
C_c=
\begin{bmatrix}
\bar b_0&\bar b_1&\cdots&\bar b_{n-1}
\end{bmatrix}
\qquad
D_c=b_n
$$

Since the denominator $A(s)$ is unchanged, the state equation remains the same; only $C_c$ and $D_c$ change.

##### Observable Canonical Form (OCF)

For both cases, use the same companion matrix as in CCF,

$$
A_o=A_c=
\begin{bmatrix}
0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\\
-a_0&-a_1&-a_2&\cdots&-a_{n-1}
\end{bmatrix}
\qquad
C_o=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}
$$

**For $m<n$.** With $b_i=0$ for $i>m$, define

$$
\beta_0=0
\qquad
\beta_k=b_{n-k}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{n-j}\beta_{k-j},
\qquad k=1,2,\ldots,n
$$

$$
B_o=
\begin{bmatrix}
\beta_1\\
\beta_2\\
\vdots\\
\beta_n
\end{bmatrix}
\qquad
D_o=0
$$

**For $m=n$.** Define

$$
\beta_0=b_n
\qquad
\beta_k=b_{n-k}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{n-j}\beta_{k-j},
\qquad k=1,2,\ldots,n
$$

$$
B_o=
\begin{bmatrix}
\beta_1\\
\beta_2\\
\vdots\\
\beta_n
\end{bmatrix}
\qquad
D_o=\beta_0=b_n
$$

**Derivation.** Write the input-output differential equation as

$$
y^{(n)}+a_{n-1}y^{(n-1)}+\cdots+a_1\dot y+a_0y
=b_nu^{(n)}+b_{n-1}u^{(n-1)}+\cdots+b_1\dot u+b_0u
$$

where $b_i=0$ above the numerator degree. Introduce the coefficients $\beta_0,\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_n$ and choose

$$
x_1=y-\beta_0u
$$

$$
x_{k+1}=\dot x_k-\beta_ku,
\qquad k=1,2,\ldots,n-1
$$

with the auxiliary variable

$$
x_{n+1}=\dot x_n-\beta_nu
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot x_1&=x_2+\beta_1u\\
\dot x_2&=x_3+\beta_2u\\
&\ \vdots\\
\dot x_{n-1}&=x_n+\beta_{n-1}u\\
\dot x_n&=x_{n+1}+\beta_nu
\end{aligned}
$$

and

$$
y=x_1+\beta_0u
$$

Substituting the recursively defined states into the input-output differential equation and comparing the state terms gives

$$
x_{n+1}=-a_0x_1-a_1x_2-\cdots-a_{n-1}x_n
$$

Therefore,

$$
A_o=A_c=
\begin{bmatrix}
0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\\
-a_0&-a_1&-a_2&\cdots&-a_{n-1}
\end{bmatrix}
\qquad
C_o=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}
$$

**For $m<n$,** set $b_i=0$ for $i=m+1,\ldots,n$. Comparing the coefficients of the input derivatives gives

$$
\beta_0=0
$$

$$
\beta_k=b_{n-k}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{n-j}\beta_{k-j},
\qquad k=1,2,\ldots,n
$$

Hence,

$$
B_o=
\begin{bmatrix}
\beta_1\\
\beta_2\\
\vdots\\
\beta_n
\end{bmatrix}
\qquad
D_o=0
$$

**For $m=n$,** comparing the coefficient of $u^{(n)}$ first gives

$$
\beta_0=b_n
$$

and the remaining coefficients give

$$
\beta_k=b_{n-k}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{n-j}\beta_{k-j},
\qquad k=1,2,\ldots,n
$$

Hence,

$$
B_o=
\begin{bmatrix}
\beta_1\\
\beta_2\\
\vdots\\
\beta_n
\end{bmatrix}
\qquad
D_o=\beta_0=b_n
$$

Since the denominator $A(s)$ is unchanged, $A_o$ and $C_o$ remain the same; only $B_o$ and $D_o$ depend on the numerator coefficients.

##### Example

**eg.** Consider

$$
y^{(3)}+5\ddot y+7\dot y+3y=\dot u+2u
$$

$$
y^{(3)}+5\ddot y+7\dot y+3y=\ddot u+3\dot u+2u
$$

With zero initial conditions,

$$
G_1(s)=\frac{s+2}{s^3+5s^2+7s+3}
\qquad
G_2(s)=\frac{s^2+3s+2}{s^3+5s^2+7s+3}
$$

Both satisfy $m<n$. The second transfer function has the common factor

$$
G_2(s)=\frac{(s+1)(s+2)}{(s+1)^2(s+3)}
$$

so a third-order realization formed directly from the uncanceled differential equation is nonminimal. Using the common unreduced denominator, their controllable canonical forms have the same

$$
A_c=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&1\\-3&-7&-5\end{bmatrix}
\qquad
B_c=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}
$$

For $G_1(s)$,

$$
C_{c1}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1&0\end{bmatrix}
\qquad D_{c1}=0
$$

and for $G_2(s)$,

$$
C_{c2}=\begin{bmatrix}2&3&1\end{bmatrix}
\qquad D_{c2}=0
$$

Therefore the two CCF state equations are identical because $A_c$ and $B_c$ are constructed from the same unreduced denominator. The different numerators appear only in the output equations.

For OCF, both realizations have

$$
A_o=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&1\\-3&-7&-5\end{bmatrix}
\qquad
C_o=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\end{bmatrix}
\qquad D_o=0
$$

For $G_1(s)$,

$$
\beta_0=0
\qquad
\beta_1=0
\qquad
\beta_2=1
\qquad
\beta_3=-3
$$

$$
B_{o1}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\-3\end{bmatrix}
$$

For $G_2(s)$,

$$
\beta_0=0
\qquad
\beta_1=1
\qquad
\beta_2=-2
\qquad
\beta_3=5
$$

$$
B_{o2}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\\5\end{bmatrix}
$$

### Change of State Variables

##### Nonsingular State Transformation

A state-space representation is not unique. Let $P$ be nonsingular and define the transformed state by

$$
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}=P^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}
\qquad
\boldsymbol{x}=P\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
$$

Substitution into

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=A\boldsymbol{x}+B\boldsymbol{u}
\qquad
\boldsymbol{y}=C\boldsymbol{x}+D\boldsymbol{u}
$$

gives

$$
\dot{\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}}=P^{-1}AP\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+P^{-1}B\boldsymbol{u}
\qquad
\boldsymbol{y}=CP\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+D\boldsymbol{u}
$$

Hence

$$
\bar A=P^{-1}AP
\qquad
\bar B=P^{-1}B
\qquad
\bar C=CP
\qquad
\bar D=D
$$

The transformed model describes the same input-output behavior when $\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}(0)=P^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}(0)$.

Any nonsingular state transformation produces an equivalent state-space representation. The converse need not hold for arbitrary nonminimal realizations; two minimal realizations of the same transfer function are related by a nonsingular state transformation.

##### Similarity and Eigenvalue Invariance

The matrices $A$ and $\bar A=P^{-1}AP$ are **similar**. Their characteristic polynomials are identical because

$$
\det(\lambda I-\bar A)=\det\left(P^{-1}(\lambda I-A)P\right)=\det(\lambda I-A)
$$

Therefore similar state matrices have the same eigenvalues, including algebraic multiplicities. Thus the eigenvalues of the state matrix are invariant under a nonsingular state transformation.

### Modal Form

##### Diagonal Modal Form

Suppose $A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}$ has distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$ with associated eigenvectors $\boldsymbol{v}_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_n$. The eigenvectors are linearly independent, so the **modal matrix**

$$
M=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}_1&\boldsymbol{v}_2&\cdots&\boldsymbol{v}_n\end{bmatrix}
$$

is nonsingular. Since

$$
AM=M\Lambda\qquad \Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\ldots,\lambda_n)
$$

$$
\Lambda=M^{-1}AM
$$

With the modal coordinates $\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}=M^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}$, the state equation becomes

$$
\dot{\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}}=\Lambda\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+M^{-1}B\boldsymbol{u}
$$

The state dynamics are decoupled because $\Lambda$ is diagonal. If $\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}_i^{T}$ is the $i$th row of $M^{-1}B$,

$$
\dot{\bar x}_i=\lambda_i\bar x_i+\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}_i^{T}\boldsymbol{u}
$$

A repeated eigenvalue does not by itself prevent diagonalization; diagonalization is possible whenever $A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors.

##### Companion Matrix and Vandermonde Matrix

For the monic polynomial $p(s)=s^n+a_{n-1}s^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1s+a_0$, the **companion matrix** is

$$
A_c=\begin{bmatrix}
0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\\
-a_0&-a_1&-a_2&\cdots&-a_{n-1}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Its characteristic polynomial is

$$
\det(sI-A_c)=p(s)
$$

In the controllable canonical form, this companion matrix is used with $B_c=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&1\end{bmatrix}^{T}$.

Let $\lambda_i$ be an eigenvalue of the companion matrix $A_c$. Then an associated eigenvector can be chosen as

$$
\boldsymbol{v}_i=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&\lambda_i&\lambda_i^2&\cdots&\lambda_i^{n-1}
\end{bmatrix}^{T}
$$

If $A_c$ has distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$ then its modal matrix is the Vandermonde matrix

$$
M_c=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}_1&\boldsymbol{v}_2&\cdots&\boldsymbol{v}_n\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}
1&1&\cdots&1\\
\lambda_1&\lambda_2&\cdots&\lambda_n\\
\lambda_1^2&\lambda_2^2&\cdots&\lambda_n^2\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\lambda_1^{n-1}&\lambda_2^{n-1}&\cdots&\lambda_n^{n-1}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

**Proof.** Since $\lambda_i$ is an eigenvalue
$$
p(\lambda_i)=\lambda_i^n+a_{n-1}\lambda_i^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1\lambda_i+a_0=0
$$

Thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_c\boldsymbol{v}_i
&=\begin{bmatrix}
\lambda_i\\
\lambda_i^2\\
\vdots\\
\lambda_i^{n-1}\\
-a_0-a_1\lambda_i-\cdots-a_{n-1}\lambda_i^{n-1}
\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}
\lambda_i\\
\lambda_i^2\\
\vdots\\
\lambda_i^{n-1}\\
\lambda_i^n
\end{bmatrix}=\lambda_i\boldsymbol{v}_i
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore $\boldsymbol{v}_i$ is an eigenvector associated with $\lambda_i$.

##### Example

**eg.** Consider

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=A\boldsymbol{x}+Bu
$$

with

$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}2&-1&-1\\0&-1&0\\0&2&1\end{bmatrix}\qquad B=\begin{bmatrix}7\\2\\3\end{bmatrix}
$$

The eigenvalues are

$$
\lambda_1=2\qquad \lambda_2=1\qquad \lambda_3=-1
$$

Choose corresponding eigenvectors

$$
\boldsymbol{v}_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}\qquad
\boldsymbol{v}_2=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}\qquad
\boldsymbol{v}_3=\begin{bmatrix}0\\-1\\1\end{bmatrix}
$$

The modal matrix is

$$
M=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}_1&\boldsymbol{v}_2&\boldsymbol{v}_3\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&0\\0&0&-1\\0&1&1\end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
\Lambda=M^{-1}AM=\begin{bmatrix}2&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&-1\end{bmatrix}\qquad
\bar B=M^{-1}B=\begin{bmatrix}2\\5\\-2\end{bmatrix}
$$

With $\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}=M^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}$,

$$
\dot{\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}}=
\begin{bmatrix}2&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&-1\end{bmatrix}\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
+\begin{bmatrix}2\\5\\-2\end{bmatrix}u
$$

### Jordan Form

##### Algebraic and Geometric Multiplicity

For an eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $A$, the **geometric multiplicity** is the dimension of its **eigenspace**
$$
E_\lambda=\ker(A-\lambda I)=\{\boldsymbol{v}\mid(A-\lambda I)\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\}
$$
It is found by solving $(A-\lambda I)\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}$ and counting the number of linearly independent eigenvectors associated with $\lambda$
$$
g_\lambda=\dim\ker(A-\lambda I)=n-\operatorname{rank}(A-\lambda I)
$$

The **algebraic multiplicity** is the multiplicity of $\lambda$ as a root of the characteristic polynomial. Compute and factor the characteristic polynomial as

$$
p_A(\mu)=\det(\mu I-A)=(\mu-\lambda)^{a_\lambda}q(\mu)\qquad q(\lambda)\neq 0
$$

Then $a_\lambda$ is the algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda$. For every eigenvalue

$$
1\leq g_\lambda\leq a_\lambda
$$

The matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if $g_\lambda=a_\lambda$ for every eigenvalue.

##### Generalized Eigenvectors

If the eigenvectors of $A$ do not form a basis, generalized eigenvectors are used. A nonzero vector $\boldsymbol{v}$ is a **generalized eigenvector** associated with an eigenvalue $\lambda$ if

$$
(A-\lambda I)^k\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\qquad (A-\lambda I)^{k-1}\boldsymbol{v}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
$$

for some positive integer $k$. The smallest such $k$ is the **grade** of $\boldsymbol{v}$. An ordinary eigenvector is therefore a generalized eigenvector of grade one.

A sequence $\boldsymbol{v}_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_r$ is a **Jordan chain** associated with $\lambda$ if

$$
(A-\lambda I)\boldsymbol{v}_1=\boldsymbol{0}\qquad
(A-\lambda I)\boldsymbol{v}_j=\boldsymbol{v}_{j-1}
\qquad j=2,\ldots,r
$$

Thus $\boldsymbol{v}_1$ is an eigenvector and $\boldsymbol{v}_j$ is a generalized eigenvector of grade $j$. Equivalently,

$$
A\boldsymbol{v}_1=\lambda\boldsymbol{v}_1\qquad
A\boldsymbol{v}_j=\boldsymbol{v}_{j-1}+\lambda\boldsymbol{v}_j
\qquad j=2,\ldots,r
$$

The vectors in a Jordan chain are linearly independent.

##### Jordan Canonical Form

Let $A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}$ have distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_q$. For $\lambda_i$, denote its algebraic and geometric multiplicities by $a_i=a_{\lambda_i}$ and $g_i=g_{\lambda_i}$, respectively, with $\sum_{i=1}^{q}a_i=n$.

A basis of generalized eigenvectors can be arranged into Jordan chains. If $M$ is the nonsingular matrix formed from these basis vectors, then

$$
AM=MJ
\qquad
J=M^{-1}AM=\operatorname{diag}(J_1,J_2,\ldots,J_q)
$$

The matrix $J$ is the **Jordan canonical form** of $A$, where $J_i$ contains all Jordan blocks associated with $\lambda_i$:

$$
J_i=\operatorname{diag}\left(J_{r_{i1}}(\lambda_i),J_{r_{i2}}(\lambda_i),\ldots,J_{r_{ig_i}}(\lambda_i)\right)
$$

Each **Jordan block** has the form

$$
J_r(\lambda)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\lambda&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\lambda&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\lambda&1\\
0&0&\cdots&0&\lambda
\end{bmatrix}
$$

For each eigenvalue $\lambda_i$,

$$
g_i=\text{number of Jordan blocks associated with }\lambda_i
\qquad
\sum_{j=1}^{g_i}r_{ij}=a_i
$$

Thus $J_i\in\mathbb{C}^{a_i\times a_i}$, and the total number of Jordan blocks is $\sum_{i=1}^{q}g_i$. Each block corresponds to one Jordan chain, and its size equals the length of that chain. For a chain $\boldsymbol{v}_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_r$,

$$
M_r=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}_1&\boldsymbol{v}_2&\cdots&\boldsymbol{v}_r\end{bmatrix}
\qquad
AM_r=M_rJ_r(\lambda)
$$

For the state equation $\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=A\boldsymbol{x}+B\boldsymbol{u}$, the transformation $\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}=M^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}$ gives

$$
\dot{\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}}=J\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\bar B\boldsymbol{u}
\qquad
\bar B=M^{-1}B
$$

Different Jordan blocks are decoupled. Within a block $J_r(\lambda)$,

$$
\dot z_1=\lambda z_1+z_2\qquad
\dot z_2=\lambda z_2+z_3\qquad
\cdots\qquad
\dot z_r=\lambda z_r
$$

so coupling remains only within a Jordan block of size greater than one. The matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if every Jordan block has size one, equivalently $g_i=a_i$ for every eigenvalue.

### Transfer Matrices and Interconnected Systems

##### Transfer Matrix from a State-Space Model

For a linear time-invariant system with zero initial conditions, the **transfer matrix** $G(s)$ is defined by

$$
\boldsymbol{Y}(s)=G(s)\boldsymbol{U}(s)
$$

where $G(s)\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times m}$. Its $(i,j)$ entry is the transfer function from the $j$th input to the $i$th output with all other inputs set to zero.

For

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=A\boldsymbol{x}+B\boldsymbol{u}\qquad
\boldsymbol{y}=C\boldsymbol{x}+D\boldsymbol{u}
$$

the Laplace transform with $\boldsymbol{x}(0)=\boldsymbol{0}$ gives

$$
s\boldsymbol{X}(s)=A\boldsymbol{X}(s)+B\boldsymbol{U}(s)
$$

$$
\boldsymbol{X}(s)=(sI-A)^{-1}B\boldsymbol{U}(s)
$$

$$
\boldsymbol{Y}(s)=C\boldsymbol{X}(s)+D\boldsymbol{U}(s)
=\left[C(sI-A)^{-1}B+D\right]\boldsymbol{U}(s)
$$

Therefore

$$
G(s)=C(sI-A)^{-1}B+D
$$

Since

$$
(sI-A)^{-1}
=\frac{1}{s}\left(I-\frac{A}{s}\right)^{-1}
=\frac{1}{s}I+\frac{1}{s^2}A+O\left(\frac{1}{s^3}\right)
$$

we have

$$
G(s)=D+\frac{1}{s}CB+\frac{1}{s^2}CAB+O\left(\frac{1}{s^3}\right)
$$

Thus $G(s)$ is a **proper rational matrix**. If $D=0$, $G(s)$ is **strictly proper**; if $D\neq 0$, it is proper but not strictly proper. In particular,

$$
\lim_{s\to\infty}G(s)=D
$$

A given zero-state input-output map has a unique transfer matrix although its state-space realization is not unique. In particular, under the nonsingular state transformation

$$
\bar A=P^{-1}AP\qquad
\bar B=P^{-1}B\qquad
\bar C=CP\qquad
\bar D=D
$$

the transformed realization has

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar G(s)
&=\bar C(sI-\bar A)^{-1}\bar B+\bar D\\
&=CP\left[P^{-1}(sI-A)P\right]^{-1}P^{-1}B+D\\
&=C(sI-A)^{-1}B+D\\
&=G(s)
\end{aligned}
$$

##### State-Space Models of Interconnected Systems

Consider two subsystems

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Sigma_1:\quad
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}_1&=A_1\boldsymbol{x}_1+B_1\boldsymbol{u}_1&
\boldsymbol{y}_1&=C_1\boldsymbol{x}_1+D_1\boldsymbol{u}_1\\
\Sigma_2:\quad
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}_2&=A_2\boldsymbol{x}_2+B_2\boldsymbol{u}_2&
\boldsymbol{y}_2&=C_2\boldsymbol{x}_2+D_2\boldsymbol{u}_2
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\boldsymbol{u}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{m_i}$ and $\boldsymbol{y}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{p_i}$, with transfer matrices

$$
G_i(s)=C_i(sI-A_i)^{-1}B_i+D_i
$$

For an interconnection, first check that each connected output and input have the same dimension, write the input-output signal relations, and then collect the states as

$$
\boldsymbol{x}=
\begin{bmatrix}
\boldsymbol{x}_1\\
\boldsymbol{x}_2
\end{bmatrix}
$$

**Parallel connection.** Let

$$
\boldsymbol{u}_1=\boldsymbol{u}_2=\boldsymbol{u}\qquad
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{y}_1+\boldsymbol{y}_2
$$

which requires

$$
m_1=m_2\qquad p_1=p_2
$$

Then

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
A_1&0\\
0&A_2
\end{bmatrix}
\boldsymbol{x}
+
\begin{bmatrix}
B_1\\
B_2
\end{bmatrix}
\boldsymbol{u}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol{y}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
C_1&C_2
\end{bmatrix}
\boldsymbol{x}
+
(D_1+D_2)\boldsymbol{u}
$$

and

$$
G(s)=G_1(s)+G_2(s)
$$

**Series connection.** Let $\Sigma_1$ be followed by $\Sigma_2$

$$
\boldsymbol{u}_1=\boldsymbol{u}\qquad
\boldsymbol{u}_2=\boldsymbol{y}_1\qquad
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{y}_2
$$

which requires

$$
p_1=m_2
$$

Then

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
A_1&0\\
B_2C_1&A_2
\end{bmatrix}
\boldsymbol{x}
+
\begin{bmatrix}
B_1\\
B_2D_1
\end{bmatrix}
\boldsymbol{u}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol{y}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
D_2C_1&C_2
\end{bmatrix}
\boldsymbol{x}
+
D_2D_1\boldsymbol{u}
$$

and

$$
G(s)=G_2(s)G_1(s)
$$

**Negative-feedback connection.** Let $\Sigma_1$ be the forward subsystem and $\Sigma_2$ the feedback subsystem

$$
\boldsymbol{u}_1=\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{y}_2\qquad
\boldsymbol{u}_2=\boldsymbol{y}_1\qquad
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{y}_1
$$

which requires

$$
p_1=m_2\qquad p_2=m_1
$$

When direct-feedthrough terms are present, the feedback interconnection is well-posed if $I+D_2D_1$, equivalently $I+D_1D_2$, is nonsingular. Define

$$
E=(I+D_2D_1)^{-1}\qquad
F=(I+D_1D_2)^{-1}
$$

Then

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
A_1-B_1ED_2C_1&-B_1EC_2\\
B_2FC_1&A_2-B_2FD_1C_2
\end{bmatrix}
\boldsymbol{x}
+
\begin{bmatrix}
B_1E\\
B_2FD_1
\end{bmatrix}
\boldsymbol{u}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol{y}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
FC_1&-FD_1C_2
\end{bmatrix}
\boldsymbol{x}
+
FD_1\boldsymbol{u}
$$

and

$$
G(s)
=
\left[I+G_1(s)G_2(s)\right]^{-1}G_1(s)
=
G_1(s)\left[I+G_2(s)G_1(s)\right]^{-1}
$$

For MIMO systems the order of the transfer-matrix products must be preserved.

### Discrete-Time, Time-Varying, and Nonlinear State-Space Models

##### Discrete-Time State-Space Models

A discrete-time linear time-invariant system is represented by the first-order difference equations

$$
\boldsymbol{x}[k+1]=A\boldsymbol{x}[k]+B\boldsymbol{u}[k]\qquad
\boldsymbol{y}[k]=C\boldsymbol{x}[k]+D\boldsymbol{u}[k]
$$

##### Time-Varying State-Space Models

A continuous-time linear time-varying system has the form

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)=A(t)\boldsymbol{x}(t)+B(t)\boldsymbol{u}(t)\qquad
\boldsymbol{y}(t)=C(t)\boldsymbol{x}(t)+D(t)\boldsymbol{u}(t)
$$

##### Nonlinear State-Space Models

A nonlinear time-varying system has the form

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u},t)\qquad
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u},t)
$$

A nonlinear time-invariant system has the form

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u})\qquad
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u})
$$

For the time-invariant system, let $(\boldsymbol{x}_0,\boldsymbol{u}_0)$ be an equilibrium operating point

$$
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_0,\boldsymbol{u}_0)=\boldsymbol{0}\qquad
\boldsymbol{y}_0=\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x}_0,\boldsymbol{u}_0)
$$

Define the deviations

$$
\delta\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_0\qquad
\delta\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_0\qquad
\delta\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_0
$$

Retaining only the first-order terms of the Taylor expansion gives

$$
\delta\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=A\delta\boldsymbol{x}+B\delta\boldsymbol{u}\qquad
\delta\boldsymbol{y}=C\delta\boldsymbol{x}+D\delta\boldsymbol{u}
$$

where
$$
A=\left.\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{f}}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\right|_{(\boldsymbol{x}_0,\boldsymbol{u}_0)}
\qquad
B=\left.\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{f}}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}\right|_{(\boldsymbol{x}_0,\boldsymbol{u}_0)}
\qquad
C=\left.\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{g}}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\right|_{(\boldsymbol{x}_0,\boldsymbol{u}_0)}
\qquad
D=\left.\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{g}}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}\right|_{(\boldsymbol{x}_0,\boldsymbol{u}_0)}
$$
